The other player, Bob, is a newcomer and has no friends.   Going by @FateNanoha 's terminology of 'error' and assuming that both players are committing an equal number of errors (along the lines of: exerting 3 ink instead of 2 ink for a Nani when Grandmother Willow is in play, etc.), do you think it is fair for that spectator to exercise her right to call a judge only for Alice's errors -- or selectively so --  and not for Bob's?  Of course, if you're the spectator, you can say, "I would call it out for both players, similarly, regardless friend or not." You can even be completely honest about that. I understand that not all errors are the same, and I'm not suggesting Bob is also only exerting 3 ink instead of 2 ink for a Nani when Grandmother Willow is in play. He is likely committing different errors. But from Bob's point of view, when both of them are making errors, but only ones beneficial to Alice result in the judge summons, how do you think he feels?  More importantly than how Bob feels, how do we prevent that spectator from exercising her right to call a judge only for errors beneficial to her friend Alice and not errors beneficial to Bob? Anyone can say they'd do it for both (We are all the white knight, the hero, the do-no-wrong protagonist in our own minds), but some people will lie as well to protect their ego, avoid penalty, help their friend, or just claim they are paying more attention to their friend's plays. Short version:
The goals of Correcting Errors are primarily to mitigate advantage and preserve tournament integrity, in that order.
You cannot, intrinsically, undo most errors. There has already been irrecoverable changes, in players minds, and often, to the game state. 
Therefore, we err on the side of removing possible advantages that could be gained by a theoretical bad actor, sometimes explicitly changing the game state further to do so.

So. I'm going to go back to an earlier point.  The spirit of play correction is not to attempt to return the game to its 'correct' state. Sometimes the remedy will move towards that, but that is not the ultimate goal. The goal is to minimize advantage. Notably, only a rewind is returning the game to a prior state. In any remedy that does not include a rewind, we are not attempting to return the game to the way it was. Non-rewind remedies are performed 'in-place'. We are changing from the current, erroneous state, to a less advantageous, but still erroneous state. This movement is advancing the game (or, maybe a lateral movement, depending on how you look at it). It is not taking it back to where it was.   So, when we perform the remedy for CCE, in this case, moving a card from the player's hand to the deck, and from the deck to the inkwell, what those cards "would have been" or where they "should have" come from is, IMO, irrelevant.
